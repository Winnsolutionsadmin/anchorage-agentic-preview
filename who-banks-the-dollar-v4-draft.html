00 · Who Banks the Dollar

Foreword

Foreword. Between the late 1830s and the Civil War, an American merchant receiving an unfamiliar out-of-town bank note often had to determine what it was actually worth. Hundreds of state-chartered banks printed their own money. Discounts on distant notes ran as high as 20 percent, and the data behind them carries this paper's whole argument: in Gary Gorton's study of the era's note prices, distance explained roughly 11 percent of the variance in discounts. The other 89 percent was the credit of the issuing bank. The market priced the bank, not the miles. Counterfeiting added another layer of uncertainty; the Secret Service's own institutional history puts counterfeit notes at nearly one-third of the currency in circulation by the end of the Civil War, an estimate historians treat as directional rather than measured. Bank note reporters answered the problem with subscription newsletters. They listed discounts on distant notes and described known counterfeits, and Thompson's Bank Note and Commercial Reporter claimed, by its own count, as many as 100,000 subscribers. The practical problem was that a merchant could not treat every unfamiliar note as the same dollar.

The loss experience underneath that pricing problem is the most instructive data the era left behind. Rolnick and Weber's Federal Reserve Bank of Minneapolis research found that while 47.8 percent of free banks eventually closed, only about 15 percent redeemed their notes below par, and total noteholder losses across four states over roughly 25 years came to about $1.6 million. More telling still, loss severity varied by a factor of 6.4 across states running nearly identical statutes. Same token design, same legal wrapper, radically different outcomes for holders, driven by the quality of the collateral and the custody behind each issuer. That is the issuer layer, observed in 1850s data.

The transition did not happen in 1863 alone. The National Currency Act of 1863 and the National Bank Act of 1864 created a federally supervised national banking system and a more uniform currency, partly to help finance the Civil War. Congress imposed a 10 percent tax on state bank notes in 1865, and state bank notes all but disappeared over the following two years. Yet the Federal Reserve's own historians are careful about what actually produced par: the statute alone did not. Notes from remote banks kept trading at discounts until the 1874 reforms built a nationwide redemption apparatus and a dedicated redemption fund, and par also depended on collateral held as Treasuries at the Treasury. A private system had shown the way even earlier: the Suffolk Bank cleared New England notes at par for a cost of about 1 basis point, decades before any federal statute, and the economics of that clearing accrued to the obligation layer that did the work. The lesson, stated the way the Fed's note states it, is that common rules begin the job and redemption infrastructure plus collateral quality complete it. That lesson runs through everything below.

I run stablecoins at Anchorage Digital Bank N.A., which received its national trust bank charter from the OCC in January 2021, by conversion of a South Dakota trust company, and has operated under continuous federal supervision since. The disclosures belong up front. Anchorage has a clear commercial interest in the argument that follows. We issue stablecoins for businesses that compete directly with one another, five client-branded dollars at last count, and that operating role, rather than the interest, is what gives us a direct view of how a stablecoin's sponsor, legal issuer, reserve custodian, and blockchain infrastructure can be separate layers. One client is also a shareholder: Tether made a $100 million equity investment in Anchorage Digital in February 2026, about 2.38 percent of the company at a $4.2 billion valuation, while Anchorage Digital Bank issues Tether's USAT. Equity and the reserve chain are separate by design, and the design is disclosed program by program. U.S. Bank has been selected to provide custody services for reserves backing Anchorage-issued payment stablecoins at the program-family level, while reserve composition and sub-custody differ per program and are disclosed in each program's monthly attestation. One issuer, one underwriting framework, five brands, at least three distinct reserve architectures underneath them. Those monthly attestations already carry the CEO and CFO certification the GENIUS Act will require, prepared under the AICPA's 2025 criteria for stablecoin reporting, roughly six months before the statute's January 2027 enforcement date; the newest program's first attestation is pending publication as of this writing. Two limits on all of this deserve equal billing. The controls address custody and verification, and they do not reach commercial terms. And the underwriting framework across competing clients, one of whom is a shareholder, is uniform and examined; that is the structural answer, and this paper will not claim more than the structure supports.

The market is keeping score on which stablecoin wins. That is a real competition, and this paper is about the second one underneath it. In public debate, "issuer" often refers loosely to the company behind a stablecoin brand. The split matters. The company that owns the brand and distribution is the sponsor. The regulated entity that mints, redeems, and manages reserves is the legal issuer. Those are different jobs. This paper argues that the issuer layer is a distinct institutional market with its own scoreboard, and the market has a size: at a category float slightly above $300 billion and Circle's disclosed 3.47 percent first-quarter 2026 reserve return, the reserve income pool runs roughly $10 to $11 billion a year at 2026 rates, and by federal law none of it is payable to the people holding the dollars: the GENIUS Act bars a permitted issuer from paying "any form of interest or yield" to holders. Roughly 60 percent of that float sits outside the federal perimeter until 2028, and the category has been close to flat for three quarters, so the pool is a measurement, and this paper makes no claim that it compounds. Who divides a pool that size, under what supervision, is a market question in its own right, and only one side of it publishes an income statement: Circle decomposes its own reserve revenue into volume and rate effects to the percentage point; no token-layer tracker publishes anything comparable.

Neutrality also needs a precise meaning. It does not mean that every stablecoin is equally safe, or that a bank should ignore differences in risk. It means no house token, and one underwriting framework for every approved client brand. The economics behind that rule are the strongest version of it, and our CEO stated them in January 2025: "imagine if the dollar could be held by dozens or hundreds of banks, but there was only one company that was earning all the interest from those dollars that were held in everybody else's banks. It would be a preposterous notion" (Empire podcast, January 27, 2025).

Here is what makes that quote worth printing in 2026: the measured economics run in the opposite direction from the hypothetical, and the direction is the argument. Inside the one integrated issuer with public books, the obligation-holding layer has been losing the float to the distribution layer, year after year. Circle retained 61.0 percent of its reserve income in 2022, 49.7 percent in 2023, and 39.1 percent in 2024; between 2023 and 2024, gross reserve income rose $230.5 million while the retained share fell $60.5 million. The split is measurable to an accounting decimal: the distribution partner's own SEC filings book roughly $910 million of fiscal 2024 stablecoin revenue against the issuer's disclosed $907.9 million, a 0.3 percent gap between two registrants reporting the same dollars from opposite sides of the ledger. The only published price for the issuance function itself is thin: a contractual issuer retention band of roughly 0.9 to 19 basis points annualized, which is between 0.2 and roughly 5 percent of the float's gross yield depending on the rate you divide by. For scale, BlackRock is paid about 17 basis points net just to manage the reserves in the Circle Reserve Fund, against the 0.9 basis point contractual floor for issuing; the reserve manager's fee runs about nineteen times the issuer's floor fee. A second seller already prices the function, and prices it lower: the Global Dollar Network markets "up to 100% of the returns" on balances back to the platforms that hold them, bidding the issuer's residual toward zero.

Two more measured facts complete the picture. The issuer layer's revenue is set by the FOMC, not by the stablecoin race: Circle grew average float 39 percent year over year in the first quarter of 2026 and grew reserve income 17 percent, because the reserve return fell 66 basis points, and the sharing formula passes roughly 53 percent of any rate swing through to distribution; sequentially, reserve income fell 10.9 percent in one quarter on roughly flat circulation. And the yield ban has an observable consequence at the brand layer: PayPal pays PYUSD holders 3.7 percent in rewards, funded by PayPal, because the issuer legally cannot. Under GENIUS no holder earns yield from an issuer, so the live competition is over who earns the float and what holders get instead: services, distribution, redemption quality. Tokenized deposits answer that competition differently, by paying the depositor, and this paper takes that answer seriously in its own section below.

So neutrality is a priced position, and the price is public. A neutral issuer deliberately competes for the smallest slice of the fee stack in exchange for the right to serve every sponsor at once, with a book that has to be built on breadth. Neutral issuance keeps the brand competition open and the economics distributed with the work. The disclosures above are what that claim costs to make honestly.

01 · Who Banks the Dollar

What a treasurer asks at 2 a.m.

Start with the scoreboard as the trackers publish it. In late July 2026, CoinGecko's stablecoin category page showed USDT at roughly $184 billion and USDC near $72 billion, together about 85 percent of a category slightly above $300 billion; on the same day, four major trackers put the category total anywhere from $296.9 billion to $312.6 billion, a $15.7 billion spread on one number. Circle reported $21.5 trillion of USDC onchain transaction volume in the first quarter of 2026, a figure that, by the glossary definition in Circle's own quarterly filing, excludes Solana; the adjusted-volume literature, meanwhile, haircuts gross onchain figures by anywhere from 5x to 177x depending on what the methodology counts as real activity. None of this is a scandal. It is the point: supply is an obligation, activity is a convention, and the token-layer scoreboard is partly constructed by the parties being scored. Those figures describe distribution, usage, and liquidity, and institutions care about all three.

They do not answer the underwriting questions an institution must resolve before it can transact. And to be honest about who is asking: almost nobody yet. The AFP's 2026 liquidity survey of 309 treasury professionals found 1 percent piloting stablecoins and 9 percent exploring them, and AFP's own director of treasury practice named the GENIUS Act as the reason treasury teams need to build the muscle now. The treasurer at 2 a.m. is not a description of today's market. It is a description of who the statute is about to create, and this section is that treasurer's briefing.

Take a payments company settling cross-border flows on stablecoin rails. It will evaluate both layers. At the token layer, it will ask about market depth, chain support, counterparty acceptance, integrations, and smart-contract risk. At the issuer layer, the questions are the ones a risk committee asks. Are the reserves real, and who confirms them independently? What happens when it needs to redeem $200 million on a Sunday? Which regulator supervises the legal issuer? How are sanctions and transaction monitoring handled? And how will its auditor treat the asset at quarter end?

One 72-hour episode answers the first question better than any framework document. In March 2023, three supervised, chartered-trust issuers slipped off peg in the same three days: USDC traded as low as 86 cents, USDP at 91 cents, and GUSD at 96 cents, by the Federal Reserve's own account of the weekend. All three were fully reserved. In that one episode, and it is one episode with three coins, the deepest trough belonged to the largest coin, because it had the largest single-bank concentration in its cash reserves: $3.3 billion at Silicon Valley Bank. Nothing about that coin's share, liquidity, integrations, or chain support changed on March 10. What changed was the answer to where the reserves sat. The contagion traveled through issuer-layer machinery too: DAI, with no exposure of its own, depegged through its peg-stability module's USDC holdings, and a two-hour technical fix took roughly 48 hours to execute on governance delay. The token layer could not see the problem coming and could not fix it once it arrived.

The Sunday question deserves a real answer rather than a dramatic pause, so here is the general case, from the public record. The fastest run on record is a bank run: Silicon Valley Bank saw over $40 billion leave, roughly 85 percent of its deposit base in motion, inside one business day. The classic money-fund failure ran the other direction: Reserve Primary broke the buck on a position of about 1.2 percent of assets and still gated redemptions for months, which is the reminder that redeemability is operational, not definitional. The one live weekend test in stablecoins, March 2023, was resolved by the federal banking safety net backstopping the failed custodian, and by no mechanism at the token layer. And the international standard already treats the question as an issuer test: Basel's cryptoasset framework gates preferential bank treatment on redemption tests measured in days, with punitive capital treatment for failure. A treasurer should ask every issuer the Sunday question, ours included, and should grade the answers on operations, not on adjectives.

The auditor question stays on the list as a question, and the treasurer should know one thing walking in: FASB's crypto fair-value standard scopes a collateralized, redeemable stablecoin out by two separate criteria, so the accounting treatment turns on the issuer's terms and the reserve's structure, not on the token's ticker. The paper's thesis lands on the reader's own balance sheet: the instrument's accounting identity is set at the issuer layer.

One more honesty item belongs in the same breath. Payments, the use case this entire market is named for, run at roughly 0.7 percent to 1 percent of stablecoin volume by the Kansas City Fed's and independent industry estimates. The market being underwritten is still forming, which is exactly why its structure is being decided now.

Market share helps answer the first set of questions. It does not answer the second, and the coin that wins on share can still fail every one of them. The stablecoin race is real, but it is not the whole institutional decision.

02 · Who Banks the Dollar

A common standard can narrow the discount

Out-of-town notes traded at discounts because holders priced the credit and default risk of the issuing bank. The national banking system reduced those differences through common note design, federal supervision, bond collateral, and, decisively, the 1874 redemption apparatus. Banks kept their own names and their own balance sheets. The notes converged as money.

The GENIUS Act, signed into law on July 18, 2025, creates the modern common baseline: at least one-to-one reserves in specified liquid assets, public redemption policies, monthly reserve disclosures with executive certification, and federal or state supervision for permitted payment stablecoin issuers. The statute runs on three clocks, and the third is the one this market has barely priced. The rulemaking clock passed its one-year marker on July 18, 2026 with the agencies' rules still in motion, a schedule fact rather than a violation, since the statute names no penalty; the OCC's principal implementing rule remains at the proposed stage. The effective-date clock lands on January 18, 2027 as the practical date, because the 120-day alternative would have required final rules by September 20, 2026. And the third clock: on July 18, 2028, it becomes unlawful for a digital asset service provider to offer a payment stablecoin to a US person unless a permitted issuer issued it. That is the moment federal law stops asking which brand won and asks only who issued. A foreign-issuer path through a Treasury comparability determination exists, so the gate names no casualties. It names a question, and the question is this paper's title.

The doors into permitted issuance are not equivalent. The state-qualified path carries a $10 billion ceiling that is definitionally unavailable to roughly 84 percent of today's float; the largest brand alone runs about 18 times that ceiling. Which leaves the federal door, and a base rate from the last time American money walked through one: when the national framework arrived in the 1860s, roughly three-quarters of the exiting state banks converted their charters rather than winding down. Jobs follow mandates, and the 1850s left us the base rate.

The historical rhyme runs deeper than atmosphere. The 1863 bond-deposit mechanism shares four design properties with the GENIUS reserve: third-party custody, an eligible-asset restriction, rule-sized backing, and a direct claim for holders, though scholars, Georgetown's Adam Levitin among them, argue the modern holder priority is weaker in a live bankruptcy than it reads on paper. And the Suffolk precedent planted in the foreword pays off here: par is a clearing and redemption outcome as much as a charter outcome, it was achieved privately at a cost of about 1 basis point, and the economics accrued to the layer that held the obligations and did the clearing work. The framework should narrow differences in reserve quality, transparency, and redemption risk. Stated as a testable prediction rather than a present-tense claim: by the time the 2028 gate closes, the discount a treasurer applies across permitted issuers should compress the way note discounts compressed after 1874, and the residual spread will price exactly what Rolnick and Weber's data priced, the quality of the specific issuer. Tokens will keep differing in liquidity, redemption access, blockchain support, and operational performance. The competition among brands, issuers, and networks carries on.

03 · Who Banks the Dollar

Western Union came to a bank

The market is beginning to test whether stablecoin sponsors will separate their brands from the regulated institutions beneath them. One case matters for what it settles about legal structure, not for what it has yet moved in volume, and this section treats it that way.

Western Union chose to launch USDPT with Anchorage Digital Bank as the legal issuer, announced October 2025 and live on Solana in May 2026. Anchorage mints and redeems USDPT under federal supervision, while Western Union, the household name in this arrangement as sponsor, keeps the customer relationship and its global distribution. The program's float is deliberately small at this stage, and its first stress arrived early: between the May 31 and June 30, 2026 attestations, the token count contracted 98.6 percent in a single month, every redemption was honored, and the reserve ran over-collateralized throughout, from 106.9 percent to 566.4 percent as the book fell, with the executive certifications on both reports. A five-figure float is not adoption evidence, and this paper does not offer it as any. It is the only live redemption stress test of this exact framework on the record, and the framework passed it.

Adjacent developments show the networks keeping their own issuer-layer scoreboard: Visa reports a $7 billion annualized stablecoin settlement run rate across nine blockchains as of April 2026, and Mastercard's June 2026 expansion names six stablecoins from five issuers. Both networks are underwriting issuers and networks, publishing the roster, and letting brands compete on top. That is the issuer layer being scored by parties with no stake in which brand wins.

Now the proof cases, stated as the general pattern they belong to rather than as novelties. On the network axis, issuers hold while networks multiply as routine practice across the market's largest brands; in our own book, Tether's USAT added Celo as a second mainnet on July 29, 2026 with Anchorage Digital Bank constant as issuer, one instance of a pattern the whole industry runs. The issuer axis is the rare one, and it has three precedents with three different outcomes. Clean, under supervision: in October 2025, USDtb's issuer of record became Anchorage Digital Bank, moving the obligations from an offshore fund vehicle into an OCC-chartered national trust bank while the brand stayed Ethena's; the attested reserves stood at $1.832 billion, 99.46 percent of them in BlackRock's BUIDL fund, a single-fund concentration this paper names rather than rounds to "primarily." Opaque, pre-framework: TUSD's operating control transferred with attestation gaps that took years to surface. Fatal: in February 2023, a state regulator directed the issuer of BUSD to stop minting, and a $16.7 billion brand with its distribution fully intact wound down to under $40 million. Same maneuver, three issuer-quality outcomes, and the variance is the argument.

The quantified instance in our own book is USDGO: launched under OSL Group's brand in early 2026, it grew roughly 21.8x in 5.6 months on a fixed issuer-side chain footprint, and OSL's own release supplies the sharpest sentence on the record about the split: USDGO is "issued by Anchorage Digital Bank N.A. ... with OSL Group serving as branding operator and distributor." Honesty about precedent belongs here too: multi-brand neutral issuance predates Anchorage, and Paxos has run it for years. The differentiator this paper claims is narrower and more defensible: charter, continuous federal supervision, and the underwritability the GENIUS framework adds, which turn a bilateral arrangement into a supervised, attested, statutorily bounded structure. One more honest clause: separability can be written asymmetrically. The market's one public issuance contract discloses that, on an uncured payment default, the distribution partner could take assignment of the brand trademarks and the issuer "would be prohibited from issuing other U.S. dollar-denominated stablecoins". Separation is real, and its terms are negotiated, which is precisely why the underwriting of the issuer matters.

04 · Who Banks the Dollar

Who can operate the issuer layer?

The honest map of this field is jobs, not teams, and the observation is the Federal Reserve's before it is mine. A Fed staff note from May 2026 describes banks "increasingly positioning themselves not only as competitors but also as service providers to the stablecoin ecosystem", holding reserve assets, offering custody, and issuing tokenized deposits at once; the Fed's September 2025 senior financial officer survey found roughly 40 percent of surveyed banks planning to prioritize holding reserve assets for stablecoin issuers, and about half of large banks planning tokenized-deposit issuance. The same institution takes multiple jobs: U.S. Bank custodies reserves for Anchorage-issued stablecoins and sits inside The Clearing House's June 2026 initiative to clear and settle tokenized commercial bank money. So the taxonomy that follows describes overlapping roles, and Anchorage holds several itself: we issue for sponsors, and we sell onchain settlement infrastructure to banks, so this description comes from inside the competition.

State the bank side at its real scale, because accuracy here is what buys credibility everywhere else. The Clearing House's shared network is announced with 17 named banks and still pre-launch as of late July 2026. JPMorgan's Kinexys, by the firm's own published figures, is live at over $3 trillion cumulative and more than $7 billion in average daily volume, and Citi's token services have been commercially live since 2024. And then state what the instrument is, because the definition decides the competition: a tokenized deposit is the issuing bank's own liability, transferable inside that bank's KYC perimeter, and it structurally cannot carry a sponsor's brand. Western Union cannot issue a bank's deposit token with Western Union's name on it. The camps compete for different jobs, and the sponsor-brand job is the one the issuer layer exists to serve.

The strongest objection from the bank side deserves its full weight. The American Bankers Association warns that payment stablecoins "have the potential to disintermediate core commercial bank activity like deposit taking and lending", and the Bank Policy Institute cites a Treasury analysis of deposit outflows reaching as much as $6.6 trillion. The federal referees put the same question in a narrower band. Federal Reserve staff model loan-book contraction between $65 billion and $1.26 trillion, with the ceiling explicitly conditioned on stablecoin issuers gaining Fed master accounts, access the bank associations' own filings oppose. The White House Council of Economic Advisers estimates the statutory yield ban moves bank lending by about $2.1 billion, two hundredths of a percent. The worst case on file requires the policy outcome its own authors are lobbying against; that sentence belongs to the record, not to me. And the IMF finds markets do not currently price significant disintermediation risk. The dollar stays banked under every version of this debate, which is the paper's title paying for itself.

A sponsor entering this market in 2026 faces three observable structures, all live. Charter your own: the OCC's December 2025 conditional approvals put the largest sponsor brands on that path, and one completed it in July 2026. Join a consortium: the branded-dollar and bank-utility alliances, from the Global Dollar Network to the 17-bank clearing build. Or buy the function as a service: Stripe's Bridge runs issuance where one shared token, USDB, serves several unrelated client platforms with reserve custody outsourced. The chartered neutral-issuer model is a fourth structure with a specific contract: each sponsor gets its own branded dollar, issued by a federally supervised bank, with per-program attested reserves. Different products, different answers to the only question that matters at this layer: whose obligation is this?

Against every entrant, the honest claim is time. Token software is easy to reproduce. Between Anchorage's January 2021 charter and the first peer conditional approvals ran 59 months; to the first peer final approval, 66. The supervisory bar rose behind that lead, and the class it leads is not small: OCC-supervised uninsured national trust banks administered $7.0 trillion at year-end 2025, roughly 23 times the entire stablecoin category, which is what makes "a specialized bank chartered for exactly this work" a description of an existing supervisory regime rather than special pleading.

Our operating history is not spotless. Anchorage's history includes a 2022 OCC consent order concerning deficiencies in its Bank Secrecy Act and anti-money laundering program, which the OCC terminated in August 2025. The credible claim is that supervision produces examinations, remediation, and a public record of accountability.

The Fed banks banks. It has never banked brands. And the full record belongs next to the aphorism: Anchorage applied for a Federal Reserve master account in August 2025 and no grant has been reported; the courts have affirmed the Fed's discretion over such accounts; the Fed's proposed "skinny" payment-account alternative is capped at $1 billion; and the Fed's own staff work models issuer master-account access as the difference between the low and high ends of its lending-contraction range. The policy question is who should connect private stablecoin programs to reserves, redemption, compliance, and onchain operations. Anchorage's position, stated with its interest disclosed, is that a specialized, continuously examined bank is well suited to it, and both the Fed and the GENIUS Act leave the question open.

05 · Who Banks the Dollar

The strongest argument against us comes from our own CEO

Nathan McCauley has made the network-effects case for stablecoins on the record, in terms sharper than any competitor would use on us. On the idea of money as a social network, he said there is "a real sense where that's not just a philosophical analogy, but actually... a real force that is binding us together" (Stablecoin Summit 2025, Singapore, October 2025). At Rebank in February 2026, describing how Tether's leadership thinks about its own product, he gave the mechanism: "they think of stablecoins and Tether as fundamentally a social network. And what do we know about social networks? Is that they grow with the square of the people that are part of the network" (Rebank, February 10, 2026).

Take that mechanism seriously, because he does. If liquidity and acceptance compound with the square of participants, distribution begets distribution, and a winning coin can become the default unit every rail must carry. At that point the neutral institution underneath it risks becoming a commodity supplier to a dominant brand. The concentration data backs the concession: over 66 months, the top two coins' combined supply share drifted 2.4 percentage points, and the category's concentration index never left the range antitrust practice labels highly concentrated. I expect the top of the stablecoin market to remain concentrated, and this paper claims no erosion.

What the square law cannot deliver is a single answer to which coin won. On the same July 2026 day, the supply leader and the transfer-volume leader were different coins: USDT held roughly 2.5 times the runner-up's supply while the runner-up moved nearly twice the daily dollars, and adjusted-volume leadership has flipped exactly once in six years. Supply share is sticky. Activity share moves. And issuance mandates move by a third mechanism entirely: supervision. The largest concentration event in the modern record was not a brand losing a competition. It was a regulator directing an issuer to stop minting, after which a $16.7 billion brand wound down 99.77 percent over 41 months with its exchange distribution fully intact, reserves whole, and redemptions honored. The square law's strongest possible case could not save the brand once the issuer layer stopped.

Concentration at the token layer settles the token question and leaves the issuer question standing. Reserve management, redemption, compliance, and regulatory accountability are jobs. Jobs follow mandates, and mandates move by choice, as USDtb showed, and by force, as BUSD showed. The network-effect case may well be right about which stablecoin retail users hold in five years. The question of who legally issues that token, holds or administers its reserves, operates redemption, and carries the compliance obligations stays open under every version of that outcome, and for flows subject to transaction monitoring, sanctions controls, and potential suspicious activity reporting obligations, both the token and the institution matter.

The hardest counterargument is still consolidation, and the honest response now has a date attached. The 1865 tax drove state notes out; the GENIUS framework works differently, leaving every brand standing and letting the market decide how many coexist. Even if one or two tokens dominate, a sponsor can still place legal issuance and reserve operations with a regulated institution, on negotiated terms that can cut in either direction, as the trademark-assignment clause above shows. Neutrality requires only that the sponsor and issuer layers remain separable, and separability is the part already demonstrated.

06 · Who Banks the Dollar

What changed my mind

When Anchorage brought USDtb onshore in October 2025, we marketed it as America's first federally regulated stablecoin. That was Anchorage's characterization and our competitive positioning. And there is a second piece of history that belongs in the open, because it is the origin of the neutrality this paper argues for. In November 2024, Anchorage co-founded the Global Dollar Network, a consortium built around one branded dollar with a published pass-the-economics-through model. Then the pipeline outgrew the membership. At Consensus Miami in May 2026, McCauley put Anchorage's issuance pipeline at as many as twenty institutional and large technology issuers, and the issued roster itself moved from three brands to five in roughly eight weeks this spring. A bank issuing for five competing sponsors cannot also belong to one brand's alliance, so Anchorage stepped back from the consortium in May 2026, in what McCauley described to CoinDesk as "increased neutrality". We did not arrive at neutrality as an abstract philosophy. We arrived at it by operating multiple client programs and discovering that a house view on which coin should win was incompatible with serving them, and we paid for the discovery by leaving an alliance we helped found. Our issuance platform is explicitly a white-label service for institutions launching their own branded dollars, or, in the words of a client's own launch release, a structure where the bank issues and the client serves as branding operator and distributor. A multi-client issuer has to separate its infrastructure role from its clients' brand competition.

07 · Who Banks the Dollar

So who banks the dollar?

Every sponsor and issuer in this market is building something real, and the stablecoins with the largest share have earned it by solving distribution problems Anchorage did not have to solve. Everyone in this paper is right about the importance of liquidity and acceptance.

The question is where each risk sits. Liquidity and acceptance live largely at the token layer. Reserves, redemption, compliance, and legal accountability live at the issuer layer, inside a supervisory regime that already administers trillions. Precedent opens the argument, and the 2028 clock closes it: the market structure gets decided now because on July 18, 2028, federal law stops asking which brand won and asks only who issued.

Anchorage Digital Bank today is the issuer of record for five client-branded dollar stablecoins, at last count: USDPT for Western Union, USDtb for Ethena, USDGO for OSL Group, USAT for Tether, and fUSD for Falcon Finance: five brands across six networks, per the programs' attestations, under one underwriting framework and the national trust bank charter held since January 2021. Sized honestly, that book is about one percent of the category on any tracker, and the largest single brand in it runs about a tenth of the state-regime ceiling. Which is the point stated as a bet rather than a boast: at this book size, a federal charter is not a compliance necessity. It is a positioning choice made roughly a decade of growth ahead of the point where the statute makes it compulsory. One gap stays conceded in the same spirit: a paper that prices the issuance function to the basis point publishes no economics for its own five programs, because none are public, and the reader should weigh that silence exactly as skeptically as we weighed everyone else's.

This establishes an early position, and early positions have to be defended. The entry the last edition of this paper predicted has already happened: the OCC granted conditional national trust charters to a wave of major sponsor brands in December 2025, and the first of them reached final approval on July 10, 2026, 59 to 66 months behind the charter this bank has operated since January 2021. Everything Anchorage has built can eventually be replicated. Supervisory relationships, compliance systems, reserve and redemption processes, technical integrations, and client trust take time to build, and time is the honest name of the advantage. Durability depends on execution, and on sponsors continuing to value independent issuance.

What the merchant in 1850 wanted was confidence that an unfamiliar note would be accepted at face value. Institutions moving digital dollars in 2026 ask a broader version of the same question. What is the token's liquidity? Who is legally responsible for it? What backs it, and how can it be redeemed? The stablecoin race answers the first question. The regulated issuer layer answers the rest, on a clock that is already running. The market structure worth wanting keeps both competitions alive: brands competing for holders in the open, and accountable institutions underneath them, examined continuously, separable by contract, and chosen on their record. The dollar found its bank once before. The brands are finding theirs now.
