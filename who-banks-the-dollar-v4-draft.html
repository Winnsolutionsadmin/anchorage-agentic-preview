00 · Who Banks the Dollar

Foreword

Foreword. Between the late 1830s and the Civil War, an American merchant receiving an unfamiliar out-of-town bank note often had to determine what it was actually worth. Hundreds of state-chartered banks printed their own money. Local notes usually traded at face value, while distant notes could trade at discounts reflecting redemption costs, distance, and confidence in the issuing bank. Counterfeiting added another layer of uncertainty. By the 1860s, an estimated one-third of the currency in circulation was counterfeit, a figure attributed to a Treasury estimate of the period. Bank note reporters answered the problem with a subscription newsletter. They listed discounts on distant notes and described known counterfeits, and Thompson's Bank Note and Commercial Reporter claimed as many as 100,000 subscribers. The practical problem was that a merchant could not treat every unfamiliar note as the same dollar.

The transition did not happen in 1863 alone. The National Currency Act of 1863 and the National Bank Act of 1864 created a federally supervised national banking system and a more uniform currency, partly to help finance the Civil War. Congress imposed a 10 percent tax on state bank notes in 1865, and state bank notes all but disappeared over the following two years. National bank notes were secured by U.S. government bonds deposited with the Treasury. The lesson is that common rules, credible backing, and accountable redemption reduced the need to price each note separately.

I run stablecoins at Anchorage Digital Bank N.A., which received its national trust bank charter from the OCC in January 2021 and has operated under continuous federal supervision since. Three disclosures belong up front. Anchorage has a clear commercial interest in the argument that follows. We now issue stablecoins for businesses that compete directly with one another, five client-branded dollars at last count, and that operating role, rather than the interest, is what gives us a direct view of how a stablecoin's sponsor, legal issuer, reserve custodian, and blockchain infrastructure can be separate layers. And one client is also a shareholder. Tether made a $100 million equity investment in Anchorage Digital at a $4.2 billion valuation in February 2026, while Anchorage Digital Bank issues Tether's USAT. Equity and the reserve chain are separate by design: U.S. Bank has been selected to provide custody services for reserves backing Anchorage-issued payment stablecoins, and USAT publishes monthly third-party reserve attestations. A shareholder holds equity in the issuer. The reserves answer to custodians, accountants, and examiners.

The market is keeping score on which stablecoin wins. That is a real competition, and this paper is about the second one underneath it. In public debate, "issuer" often refers loosely to the company behind a stablecoin brand. The split matters. The company that owns the brand and distribution is the sponsor. The regulated entity that mints, redeems, and manages reserves is the legal issuer. Those are different jobs. This paper argues that the issuer layer is a distinct institutional market with its own scoreboard.

Neutrality also needs a precise meaning. It does not mean that every stablecoin is equally safe, or that a bank should ignore differences in risk. It means no house token, and one underwriting framework for every approved client brand. The economics behind that rule are the strongest version of it, and our own CEO stated them in January 2025: "imagine if the dollar could be held by dozens or hundreds of banks, but there was only one company that was earning all the interest from those dollars that were held in everybody else's banks. It would be a preposterous notion" (Empire podcast, January 27, 2025). Neutral issuance keeps the brand competition open and the economics distributed with the work. The disclosures above are what that claim costs to make honestly.

01 · Who Banks the Dollar

What a treasurer asks at 2 a.m.

In late July 2026, CoinGecko's stablecoin category tracker showed USDT at roughly $184 billion and USDC near $72 billion, together about 85 percent of a category slightly above $300 billion. Circle reported $21.5 trillion of USDC onchain transaction volume in the first quarter of 2026. Those figures describe distribution, usage, and liquidity. Institutions care about all three.

They do not answer the underwriting questions an institution must resolve before it can transact.

Take a payments company settling cross-border flows on stablecoin rails. It will evaluate both layers. At the token layer, it will ask about market depth, chain support, counterparty acceptance, integrations, and smart-contract risk. At the issuer layer, the questions are the ones a risk committee asks. Are the reserves real, and who confirms them independently? What happens when it needs to redeem $200 million on a Sunday? Which regulator supervises the legal issuer? How are sanctions and transaction monitoring handled? And how will its auditor treat the asset at quarter end?

Market share helps answer the first set of questions. It does not answer the second, and the coin that wins on share can still fail every one of them. The stablecoin race is real, but it is not the whole institutional decision.

02 · Who Banks the Dollar

A common standard can narrow the discount

Out-of-town notes traded at discounts because redemption could be costly and because holders priced the credit and default risk of the issuing bank. The national banking system reduced those differences through common note design, federal supervision, bond collateral, and a federal redemption framework. Banks kept their own names and their own balance sheets. The notes converged as money.

The GENIUS Act, signed into law on July 18, 2025, creates a common statutory baseline for permitted payment stablecoin issuers. Once effective and implemented, it requires at least one-to-one reserves in specified liquid assets, public redemption policies, monthly reserve disclosures, and federal or state supervision. The framework opens legal issuance to three kinds of institutions: subsidiaries of insured depository institutions, federally qualified nonbank issuers, and state-qualified issuers.

Implementation continues. The statute passed its one-year rulemaking marker on July 18, 2026 with agency rules still in motion, the OCC's principal implementing rule remains at the proposed stage, and the Act takes effect on the earlier of January 18, 2027, or 120 days after the primary federal payment stablecoin regulators issue final implementing regulations. The regime is an architecture still being built.

The framework should narrow differences in reserve quality, transparency, and redemption risk, while tokens keep differing in liquidity, redemption access, blockchain support, and operational performance. The modern parallel is limited but useful: common standards spare the market a fresh credit analysis on every unit, and the competition among brands, issuers, and networks carries on.

03 · Who Banks the Dollar

Western Union came to a bank

The market is beginning to test whether stablecoin sponsors will separate their brands from the regulated institutions beneath them.

Western Union chose to launch USDPT with Anchorage Digital Bank as the legal issuer. Anchorage mints and redeems USDPT under federal supervision, while Western Union keeps the customer relationship and the distribution through its global network. That is the sponsor layer and the issuer layer separated in public, at the scale of a household name.

Adjacent developments show stablecoins entering institutional treasury and settlement workflows: the State Street Galaxy Onchain Liquidity Sweep Fund launched in May 2026 taking PYUSD for subscriptions and redemptions, subject to portfolio availability, Visa has let select issuers and acquirers settle Visa obligations onchain since December 2025, and Mastercard announced expanded settlement across several regulated stablecoins in June 2026. Each shows an institution making explicit choices about tokens, networks, and counterparties. The neutrality thesis rests on narrower evidence.

One transaction carries most of the weight. In October 2025, Anchorage Digital Bank became the sole issuer of USDtb, which remained Ethena's product and brand. Anchorage described it as the first major stablecoin, with a market capitalization close to $2 billion, to move to a new issuer. What changed was the legal issuer, the reserve arrangement, and the applicable regulatory perimeter. What held was the name on the token. USDtb's reserves sit primarily in BlackRock's BUIDL fund, apart from the issuer, under the custody arrangement described above.

Nine months later, the separation showed a second axis. On July 29, 2026, Tether's USAT launched on Celo, its second mainnet, with native mint and burn on both networks, and Anchorage Digital Bank stayed the issuer across both. USDtb showed a brand holding while the legal issuer changed. USAT shows the issuer holding while the network changes. Sponsor, issuer, and network have each now moved independently of the others in a live market.

A brand that can change legal issuers while keeping its name has an existence separate from its issuer. The two cases prove a narrow point, and the narrow point is enough: the layers separate in practice. Who captures the economics over time stays open, in the market and in this paper. The full set of relationships behind the five stablecoins Anchorage issues, sponsor, legal issuer, reserve manager, and custodian, each mapped by legal entity, is drawn in the two-layer market map that embeds here in the published version.

05 · Who Banks the Dollar

Who can operate the issuer layer?

If the issuer layer is its own market, the next question is who can operate it. The field is forming into three camps. Corporate sponsors and consortiums can build or acquire regulated issuing entities. State-qualified issuers can operate under state supervision, subject to federal standards and scale-based transition rules; against a state-based regime, Anchorage claims a single federal charter and direct OCC supervision. And large commercial banks and bank-owned infrastructure providers are developing tokenized-deposit and onchain-settlement systems: The Clearing House announced an initiative in June 2026 to clear and settle tokenized commercial bank money, still pre-launch as of late July. Anchorage sells onchain settlement infrastructure to banks as well, so this description comes from inside the competition. Against that camp, Anchorage claims focus: a bank without a proprietary stablecoin may be better positioned to serve sponsors who compete with one another.

The demand side is consolidating toward alliances. An institution coming onchain faces a choice I put to clients directly: use an existing stablecoin and let someone else eat your lunch, or issue your own. More of them are choosing to issue, and few want to build a regulated issuing entity from scratch. Second Generation Stablecoins are alliance driven. Going solo is no longer an option.

Against new entrants of every kind, the honest claim is time. Token software is easy to reproduce. A supervised operating history, tested reserve and redemption processes, and a mature compliance organization take years to assemble, and the years are the qualification.

Our operating history is not spotless. Anchorage's history includes a 2022 OCC consent order concerning deficiencies in its Bank Secrecy Act and anti-money laundering program, which the OCC terminated in August 2025. The credible claim is that supervision produces examinations, remediation, and a public record of accountability.

The Fed banks banks. It has never banked brands. It anchors central-bank money and the broader payments system, and payment stablecoins are issued by supervised private institutions operating inside that dollar system. The policy question is who should connect private stablecoin programs to reserves, redemption, compliance, and onchain operations. Anchorage's position is that a specialized bank chartered for exactly this work is particularly well suited to it. That is an argument about institutional design, and both the Fed and the GENIUS Act leave the question open.

The strongest argument against us comes from our own CEO

Nathan McCauley has made the network-effects case for stablecoins on the record, in terms sharper than any competitor would use on us. On the idea of money as a social network, he said there is "a real sense where that's not just a philosophical analogy, but actually... a real force that is binding us together" (Stablecoin Summit 2025, Singapore, October 2025). At Rebank in February 2026, describing how Tether's leadership thinks about its own product, he gave the mechanism: "they think of stablecoins and Tether as fundamentally a social network. And what do we know about social networks? Is that they grow with the square of the people that are part of the network" (Rebank, February 10, 2026).

Take that mechanism seriously, because he does. If liquidity and acceptance compound with the square of participants, distribution begets distribution, and a winning coin can become the default unit every rail must carry. At that point, the neutral institution underneath it risks becoming a commodity supplier to a dominant brand. Network effects are visible in the concentration data, and I expect the top of the stablecoin market to remain concentrated.

Concentration at the token layer settles the token question and leaves the issuer question standing. Network effects attach most strongly to liquidity, acceptance, integrations, and distribution. Reserve management, redemption, compliance, and regulatory accountability are jobs. Jobs follow mandates, and mandates can move. USDtb matters here because its legal issuer changed while its brand held, and USAT matters because its issuer held while its networks multiplied.

The historical analogy supports this narrower point. National bank notes became more uniform through common rules, federal bond security, and redemption arrangements, while each issuing bank kept its own balance sheet and its own examiners. Standardization at the money layer coexisted with competition among the institutions underneath it.

The network-effect case may well be right about which stablecoin retail users hold in five years. The question of who legally issues that token, holds or administers its reserves, operates redemption, and carries the compliance obligations stays open under every version of that outcome. For enterprise treasury, cross-border settlement, and flows subject to transaction monitoring, sanctions controls, and potential suspicious activity reporting obligations, both the token and the institution matter.

The hardest counterargument is still consolidation. The National Banking Acts and the 1865 tax drove state bank notes out of circulation over several years. The GENIUS Act works differently: it creates a common issuer framework and leaves every brand standing, and whether many brands coexist is an outcome the law leaves to the market. Even if one or two tokens dominate, a sponsor can still place legal issuance and reserve operations with a regulated institution. Neutrality requires only that the sponsor and issuer layers remain separable, and separability is the part already demonstrated.

What changed my mind

When Anchorage brought USDtb onshore in October 2025, we marketed it as America's first federally regulated stablecoin. That was Anchorage's characterization and our competitive positioning.

What changed is that the pipeline outgrew the positioning. At Consensus Miami in May 2026, McCauley put Anchorage's pipeline at as many as twenty institutional and large technology issuers, and he has described the firm as having won the large issuance mandates announced since the GENIUS Act passed (11AM w/ Seed Club, February 26, 2026). A pipeline that broad turned favoring any one client-branded coin into a conflict.

Our issuance platform is explicitly a white-label service for institutions launching their own branded dollars. McCauley described the resulting shift to CoinDesk as "increased neutrality". We did not arrive at neutrality as an abstract philosophy. We arrived at it by operating multiple client programs and discovering that a house view on which coin should win was incompatible with serving them. The business revealed a structural point. A multi-client issuer has to separate its infrastructure role from its clients' brand competition.

08 · Who Banks the Dollar

So who banks the dollar?

Every sponsor and issuer in this market is building something real, and the stablecoins with the largest share have earned it by solving distribution problems Anchorage did not have to solve. Everyone in this paper is right about the importance of liquidity and acceptance.

The question is where each risk sits. Liquidity and acceptance live largely at the token layer. Reserves, redemption, compliance, and legal accountability live at the issuer layer. The OCC's original national-currency role is a useful precedent for common standards and accountable redemption. Precedent opens the argument, and the market structure gets decided now.

Anchorage Digital Bank today is the issuer of record for five client-branded dollar stablecoins: USDPT for Western Union, USDtb for Ethena, USDGO for OSL Group, USAT for Tether, and fUSD for Falcon Finance. All five are client brands, underwritten under one framework, on issuance infrastructure spanning more than 45 blockchains, under the national trust bank charter the OCC granted in January 2021 and the continuous supervision that has run since.

This establishes an early position, and early positions have to be defended. Competitors will enter, some of them far larger, and everything Anchorage has built can eventually be replicated. Supervisory relationships, compliance systems, reserve and redemption processes, technical integrations, and client trust take time to build, and time is the honest name of the advantage. Durability depends on execution, and on sponsors continuing to value independent issuance.

What the merchant in 1850 wanted was confidence that an unfamiliar note would be accepted at face value. Institutions moving digital dollars in 2026 ask a broader version of the same question. What is the token's liquidity? Who is legally responsible for it? What backs it, and how can it be redeemed? The stablecoin race answers the first question. The regulated issuer layer answers the rest. The market structure worth wanting keeps both competitions alive: brands competing for holders in the open, and accountable institutions underneath them, examined continuously, separable by contract, and chosen on their record. The dollar found its bank once before. The brands are finding theirs now.

The bottom line

The stablecoin race answers the first question. The regulated issuer layer answers the rest. The dollar found its bank once before. The brands are finding theirs now.
